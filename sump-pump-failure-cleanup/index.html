Dealing With Sump Pump Failure Cleanup in St. Cloud

A sump pump has one job, and it tends to fail at the moment it is needed most. Heavy rain raises the water table, the pit fills, and then the power goes out, the float sticks or the pump cannot keep up. Water rises over the rim of the pit and spreads across the basement floor. Until pumping resumes, the water continues to rise.

Clean is the wrong word for what comes out of a sump pit. It has come through soil and often carries silt, bacteria and whatever was in the drain tile, so we treat it as Category 2 at best and Category 3 if it has sat or mixed with sewage. Cleanup is different as a result, with wet drywall, carpet pad and other porous materials generally removed and the rest cleaned and disinfected ahead of drying.

Your First Steps

  1. Stay Out of the WaterDo not step into a flooded basement while the power is on. Outlets, the pump cord or the furnace might already be wet and live.
  2. Turn Off the Basement CircuitsTurn off the basement circuits at the breaker panel, but only if you can reach it from a dry spot. If the panel is in the water, call the utility.
  3. Look at the Pump From a DistanceLook to see whether the pump came unplugged, its outlet tripped or the float is pinned against the pit wall. Reaching into the pit or the water is not safe.
  4. Treat the Water as ContaminatedIf going down cannot be avoided, put on gloves and rubber boots, leave pets and children upstairs, and wash up after handling wet items.
  5. Save What Is Dry, Then CallTake pictures of the pump and the water level, bring valuables that are still dry upstairs, and phone us. Waiting for it to recede without help is a mistake.
Our Process

Our Sump Pump Failure Cleanup Process

1

Get the Water Out

Because the pumps and extraction units are ours, the broken sump does not hold up the work. Low spots, the area by the pit and the rest of the floor are cleared of water and silt.

2

Take Out What Got Wet

Carpet, padding, insulation and wet drywall that the water contacted are pulled by a crew in protective gear, bagged and removed from the property so the concrete and studs are open.

3

Wash and Disinfect

An antimicrobial product goes on after the concrete, the studs and any kept belongings have been washed. This happens before drying so contaminants are not blown around the basement.

4

Dry and Confirm

Dehumidifiers and air movers run until moisture meter readings on the framing and concrete return to normal, typically 3 to 5 days. A working pump should be back in the pit before our equipment leaves.

Insurance

What About Insurance?

This is one of the most misunderstood claims. A standard homeowners policy usually excludes water that backs up or overflows from a sump, and flood insurance is a separate policy with its own rules. Often the only coverage comes from an added endorsement for water backup and sump overflow, which has limits of its own. Check the declarations page for it, or call your agent to find out. Our records of the failed pump, how high the water rose and what it damaged go into your claim file.

Common Causes

What Causes It

  • Loss of power at the pump alone, from a loose cord or a tripped circuit.
  • A pump too small for the volume of water entering the pit.
  • Outside the house, a crushed, clogged or separated discharge pipe.
  • Stuck or snagged float switches, which never tell the pump to start.
  • Gravel, silt or debris in the pit jamming the impeller or clogging the intake.
If It Is Left

What Happens if You Wait

Outlets and Wiring

Rising water can get into receptacles mounted low on basement walls. Before those circuits are used again, an electrician needs to inspect whatever was under water.

Wall Framing

The wood plate at the base of each wall sits right on the slab and soaks up water first. With the wall opened up, this wood is scrubbed, treated and then dried to normal readings.

Boxes and Stored Items

Cardboard boxes, furniture legs, books and fabrics soak up contaminated water quickly. Within days mold shows up on absorbent things that were in it, and they are difficult to disinfect.

Basement Mechanical Equipment

Mechanical equipment often shares the floor with the sump pit. Controls, wiring and burners can be destroyed by water, so every appliance needs an inspection before it runs again.
